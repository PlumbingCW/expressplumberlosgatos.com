Commercial plumbing emergency: what to do first

A flooding restroom or a backed-up kitchen drain is costing you customers while it runs, and we answer that kind of call 24/7. Before we arrive, a few quick moves limit the damage and tell us what we are walking into.

Everyone in the building should stay out of standing water near outlets or equipment.

  1. Close the building's main water valve, usually near the meter box or on the wall where the supply enters, by turning it clockwise..
  2. Switch off power to wet areas at the panel, and unplug floor-level equipment if you can do so dry..
  3. Move stock, paper and electronics off the floor, and stop using every restroom and sink on the affected drain line..
  4. Call (855) 575-2890 and tell us the building type, the floor, and whether the water is clean, gray or sewage..

If you are a tenant, call your property manager too. Some leases decide who may order emergency work, and we would rather know before we start.

How commercial plumbing differs from a house call

.

A commercial building has bigger pipe, more fixtures on one line and a lot of people sharing them. A stoppage in a ten-fixture restroom group behaves nothing like a single bathroom sink. Flow rates are higher, so a failed valve empties more water onto the floor, faster.

Retail units often share a roof, a main line or a cleanout with neighbors. Offices may have a mechanical room with a water heater, a booster pump or a tempering valve. Restaurants add floor drains, prep sinks, dishwashers and grease interceptors to that list.

Restaurant drains, grease and the district rule

.

Kitchen drains clog from grease, starch and food solids that cool and harden in the line. The cable we use first clears a stoppage, but repeat clogs usually mean grease has built up on the pipe wall, and high-pressure jetting scours it clean.

West Valley Sanitation District requires food service establishments connected to its sewer to have a grease control device approved by the district manager, and it does not allow chemical or bacteria injection ports on grease traps. Ask the district before you add or change a trap, and keep the pumping schedule written down.

Backflow testing and protection for businesses

.

A backflow assembly keeps used or contaminated water from flowing back into the clean supply when pressure drops. Businesses commonly have one on the water line, on a fire sprinkler feed, on an irrigation system or on a boiler fill.

These devices have springs, check valves and test cocks that wear or foul with debris. Testing confirms each part still seals. Which devices need testing and how often is set by the water supplier and local rules, so ask San Jose Water or the Town what applies to your building, then call us to schedule the test or repair a failed assembly.

Sewer backflow devices in low buildings

.

The sanitary district can require an owner-funded sewer backflow device when the lowest fixture floor sits less than a foot above the nearest upstream manhole, or when past backups call for one. Owners are told to check it every three to six months, because its flap can close during a blockage.

If your restroom or floor drain overflows during heavy use, that device is one of the first places to look. We check the flap, clean the housing and clear the line downstream.

Commercial leak detection, honestly

.

Some commercial water losses are traceable, and some are not worth the chase. Meter readings after hours, listening on exposed pipe and pressure tests can find a hidden supply leak in a slab or ceiling.

If the loss is small and the water bill is the only symptom, check irrigation timers and toilet flappers first. Our leak-finding method is the same one used in homes, and we will tell you if the test shows no leak to find.
